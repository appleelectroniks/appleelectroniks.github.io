Tensile Breaking Strength vs Tensile Strength: Key Differences Explained

Understand how breaking force differs from tensile stress, why fabric and solid-material results use different units, and which testing methods provide meaningful comparisons.

Material TestingBy Apple ElectroniksPublished 13 November 2026
Quick answer

Tensile breaking strength in textiles commonly refers to breaking or maximum force, measured in newtons (N). Tensile strength in engineering generally means peak tensile stress, calculated from force divided by original cross-sectional area and expressed in megapascals (MPa). Always confirm the testing standard, units, and whether a reported value means peak force or force at rupture.

What Is Tensile Breaking Strength?#

Tensile breaking strength describes how much pulling force a specimen can withstand in a test carried to failure. In textile quality control, the term commonly refers to the breaking force or maximum force, reported in newtons (N). Because standards use these terms differently, the test method and reported measurement must always be identified.

Breaking force describes a specified specimen-failure load. Standards differ in terminology: ASTM D5034-21(2025) is titled Breaking Strength and Elongation, while ASTM D5035-11(2024) uses Breaking Force and Elongation.

Is maximum force always the force at rupture?

No. A specimen might reach 480 N before rupturing at 420 N (illustrative). Reports must distinguish peak force from force at rupture. This becomes especially important when software labels both quantities simply as “breaking strength” and hides where on the force–extension curve each value occurred.

For fabrics, measured force depends on specimen width, grip arrangement and warp or weft direction. Consistency in specimen preparation is crucial because an unusually narrow or damaged strip can produce a misleading result. ISO 13934-1:2013, reviewed and confirmed in 2024, specifies a strip method for maximum force and elongation at maximum force. Its terminology is more precise than calling every output simply “tensile strength.”

What Is Tensile Strength?#

Tensile strength is the maximum engineering tensile stress reached during a specified pulling test. It is calculated by dividing maximum force by the specimen's original cross-sectional area and is commonly expressed in megapascals (MPa). This allows comparisons between suitable solid specimens of different sizes, provided their testing methods are compatible.

Ultimate tensile strength (UTS) is peak engineering tensile stress. The U.S. Department of Energy handbook explains the calculation.

UTS (MPa) = Maximum force (N) ÷ Original cross-sectional area (mm²)

Since 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa, a specimen with a peak load of 600 N and an original area of 20 mm² has a calculated UTS of 30 MPa. This is an illustrative calculation.

Is tensile stress at break the same as UTS?

Not necessarily. Tensile stress at break uses the force at rupture, whereas UTS uses the peak force. When a material continues deforming after its peak load, the values differ. This distinction is relevant to suitable plastics tested under methods such as ASTM D638-22.

Because a fabric's effective load-bearing area is difficult to define, strip tests typically report force in N. In contrast, a uniform plastic specimen can often be measured across its thickness and width to establish a suitable initial area for stress calculations.

What Is the Difference Between Tensile Breaking Strength and Tensile Strength?#

The central distinction is usually force versus stress. In textile use, tensile breaking strength commonly means a measured load in newtons; engineering tensile strength means the maximum load divided by initial cross-sectional area in MPa. However, terminology is not completely consistent, so units and the governing test standard take priority over the label.

Can 500 N be compared with 25 MPa?

No. 500 N is force, while 25 MPa is stress. For a suitable specimen with a 20 mm² area, 500 N ÷ 20 mm² = 25 MPa. Without area, conversion is impossible.

ISO 13934-1 measures fabric maximum force; the DOE handbook explains engineering stress. These outputs are not directly interchangeable.

How Do ISO and ASTM Methods Measure These Properties?#

ISO and ASTM methods define what should be measured, how specimens are held, and which units should be reported. Fabric strip and grab procedures primarily evaluate force and elongation, whereas appropriate plastic tensile tests can calculate engineering stress using the initial cross-sectional area. Different results are not automatically interchangeable.

Standard Specimen and method Main measurement
ISO 13934-1:2013 Fabric strip Maximum force; elongation at maximum force
ISO 13934-2:2014 Fabric grab Maximum force
ASTM D5034-21(2025) Fabric grab Breaking strength and elongation
ASTM D5035-11(2024) Fabric strip Breaking force and elongation
ISO 2062:2009 Single-end yarn Breaking force; elongation at break
ASTM D638-22 Specified plastics Tensile properties including stress

Why can strip and grab results differ?

A strip test grips the specimen width; a grab test clamps part of a wider specimen. Their results are not interchangeable.

ASTM D5035 is not recommended for knitted fabrics or other high-stretch textiles exceeding 11% stretch. As of November 2026, ISO 13934-1:2013 and ISO 13934-2:2014 remain current, following reviews in 2024 and 2025 respectively.

How Are Breaking Force and Tensile Strength Calculated?#

A tensile machine records force and specimen extension. Breaking or maximum force is read in newtons; engineering tensile strength requires dividing the maximum force by the original cross-sectional area. Keeping those calculations separate prevents laboratories from mistaking a load result for a stress result or confusing peak load with final rupture.

Example: Three values from one specimen

Consider a hypothetical solid-material specimen with 20 mm² original area, a maximum load of 600 N, and 500 N at rupture.

Result Calculation Value
Maximum force Direct reading 600 N
Ultimate tensile strength 600 ÷ 20 30 MPa
Engineering stress at break 500 ÷ 20 25 MPa

These values are hypothetical, not product test results.

How is elongation calculated?

Elongation (%) = (Extension ÷ Initial gauge length) × 100

For example, 16 mm extension from a 100 mm initial gauge length equals 16% elongation. Elongation at maximum force and at rupture are distinct where a test method specifies both.

The DOE handbook explains stress and strain, while ISO 13934-1 specifies fabric maximum force. Never invent fabric area to calculate MPa.

Why Do Fabrics, Yarns and Plastics Report Different Strength Units?#

Fabrics, yarns and plastics use different reporting conventions because their geometries and material structures differ. A fabric strip is often assessed by force, a yarn can be assessed by breaking force or force relative to linear density, and a suitable solid plastic specimen can be assessed using engineering stress.

Which units are appropriate?

  • Fabric: Typically N, together with specimen width, test direction and method.
  • Yarn: N or cN for force; cN/tex for tenacity.
  • Plastic: Commonly MPa when an original cross-sectional area is meaningfully defined.

Tenacity is breaking force divided by fibre or yarn linear density. A hypothetical yarn breaking at 300 cN with a linear density of 15 tex has a tenacity of 20 cN/tex. Tenacity cannot be treated as MPa without an appropriate physical conversion model.

ISO 2062:2009 addresses single-end yarn breaking force and elongation; a revision is under development.

Record fabric width, test direction, conditioning and speed. A 50 mm strip result is not interchangeable with a grab result. These methods distribute tensile load differently.

Frequently Asked Questions#

Fabric breaking strength commonly uses force; engineering tensile strength uses stress. Always compare units, methods and whether results describe peak force or rupture. The following answers also explain when yarn tenacity, maximum force and tensile stress should be reported separately, helping laboratories avoid comparing measurements that have different physical meanings.

1. Is tensile breaking strength the same as tensile strength?

Not always. Fabric results may use N, while engineering tensile strength uses MPa; check the method.

2. Which units are used for fabric breaking strength?

Usually N or cN, with specimen width and test method recorded.

3. Why is tensile strength reported in MPa?

It represents force divided by area; 1 MPa = 1 N/mm².

4. Can maximum force differ from force at rupture?

Yes. Rupture may occur after peak load, at a lower force.

5. Which standards apply to fabric tensile tests?

ISO 13934-1:2013 covers strips; ISO 13934-2:2014 covers grab tests. ASTM D5035/D5034 are related methods.

6. Can a universal testing machine measure both?

Yes, provided suitable grips, cross-sectional dimensions and test methods are used.

7. Is yarn tenacity the same as breaking force?

No. Tenacity is breaking force per linear density, typically cN/tex.

8. What should a manufacturer report for fabric quality control?

Report the prescribed force with units, width, testing direction and conditions.
