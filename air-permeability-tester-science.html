How an Air Permeability Tester Explains Airflow Through Porous Materials

Learn how pressure difference, pore structure, thickness and test conditions determine airflow through textiles, paper, nonwovens and other porous materials.

Published by Apple Electroniks Updated: September 23, 2026 Estimated reading time: 10 minutes

An Air Permeability Tester measures the volume of air passing through a defined material area under a controlled pressure difference. The result helps manufacturers quantify breathability, porosity, filtration resistance and production consistency instead of relying on subjective observations.

Air permeability is the rate at which air flows perpendicularly through a material under specified conditions of pressure, area and time.

What Causes Air to Move Through Porous Materials?

Air moves through a porous material when pressure on one side is higher than pressure on the other. The resulting pressure gradient drives air into connected pores and channels. Material thickness, open-pore dimensions, pore connectivity and air viscosity resist that movement and determine the resulting flow rate.

A porous material is a solid structure containing voids through which a fluid may potentially travel. For airflow to cross an entire specimen, some voids must form connected pathways between its two surfaces. High internal void volume does not guarantee high permeability when those voids are closed or poorly connected.

A peer-reviewed woven-fabric study evaluated pore-scale Reynolds numbers from approximately 2.4 to 76.9. The simulations used a 200 Pa pressure difference and air at 298 K, indicating laminar to weakly inertial flow through the studied structures. View the peer-reviewed fabric airflow study .

  • A higher pressure difference generally creates greater airflow.
  • Smaller and less-connected pores increase flow resistance.
  • Greater thickness produces a longer airflow path.
  • Irregular pores force air to change direction.
  • Coatings can partially or completely close open passages.

How Does an Air Permeability Tester Work?

An Air Permeability Tester clamps a specimen over a known opening, creates a specified pressure difference across it and measures the airflow required to maintain that pressure. Dividing volumetric airflow by the exposed test area produces an air-permeability result suitable for comparison with specifications or previous batches.

  1. Condition and prepare the specimen.
  2. Install the required test head or aperture.
  3. Place the specimen flat across the opening.
  4. Clamp the specimen to prevent edge leakage.
  5. Set the specified differential pressure and reporting unit.
  6. Draw or force air through the exposed area.
  7. Allow the pressure and airflow readings to stabilize.
  8. Record the result and repeat at additional locations.
Air Permeability (R) = Volumetric Airflow (Q) ÷ Test Area (A)

Pressure must accompany every reported result. A value measured at 100 Pa should not automatically be compared with a result obtained at 200 Pa.

Which Material Properties Control Air Permeability?

Air permeability is controlled principally by pore size, open-area percentage, pore connectivity, thickness and structural compression. In textiles, yarn diameter, yarn density, weave or knit geometry, fibre packing and finishing treatments collectively shape the available airflow pathways.

For woven fabrics, spaces between warp and weft yarns commonly form the principal airflow channels. Increasing the number of yarns per unit length typically reduces those openings.

One peer-reviewed investigation modelled 48 fabric configurations comprising six weave types, four set densities and two geometric approaches. Increasing set density reduced pore size and air permeability in the investigated structures.

  • Construction: Woven, knitted and nonwoven materials create different pathways.
  • Yarn or fibre density: Tighter packing normally restricts airflow.
  • Thickness: A thicker material creates a longer resistant path.
  • Coatings: Films, binders and finishes may obstruct pores.
  • Compression: Pressure can reduce thickness and void volume.
  • Moisture: Fibre swelling may change the available passages.
  • Production variation: Uneven coating or yarn spacing creates local differences.
Porosity is the proportion of a material’s volume occupied by voids, whereas permeability describes how readily air travels through connected voids.

Two products can therefore have similar total porosity but very different airflow performance. This distinction is especially important when testing closed-cell foams, membranes and coated technical fabrics.

How Are Air-Permeability Results Expressed?

Air-permeability results are usually expressed as airflow per unit area at a stated pressure difference. Common units include litres per square metre per second, cubic centimetres per square centimetre per second and millimetres per second. Gurley instruments instead report the time required for a fixed air volume to pass.

1 cm³/cm²/s = 10 L/m²/s = 10 mm/s

Unit conversion does not make results comparable when the pressure, test area, conditioning atmosphere or test apparatus differs. A complete report should include:

  • Material identification and specimen orientation
  • Applicable standard and edition
  • Exposed test area
  • Applied differential pressure
  • Individual readings and arithmetic mean
  • Measurement unit
  • Conditioning atmosphere
  • Number of test locations
  • Any leakage, deformation or test anomaly

Which Standards Apply to Air-Permeability Testing?

The applicable standard depends on the material and its intended use. ISO 9237 and ASTM D737 are widely specified for textile fabrics, while ISO 5636-5 covers the Gurley method for paper and board. Other methods apply to flexible foams, nonwovens and specialized industrial materials.

Standard Typical Material Measurement Approach Common Result
ISO 9237 Woven, knitted, nonwoven and technical fabrics Airflow through a defined area at a specified pressure Flow per unit area
ASTM D737 Textile fabrics Perpendicular airflow under a prescribed pressure difference Volumetric flow per unit area
ISO 5636-5 Paper and board Time for a defined air volume using a Gurley apparatus Gurley seconds
ASTM D3574 Flexible cellular materials Airflow through flexible foam Specified flow result
TAPPI T 251 Porous paper and related materials Air-permeance measurement Method-dependent result

ISO 9237 is an international method for measuring the permeability of fabrics to air. ISO states that the method applies to most fabrics, including industrial fabrics, nonwovens and permeable made-up textile articles.

What Is the Difference Between Direct-Flow and Gurley Testing?

Direct-flow instruments measure the volume or velocity of air crossing a specimen at controlled pressure. Gurley instruments measure how many seconds a fixed volume of air takes to pass through it. Direct-flow testing suits many textiles and nonwovens, while Gurley testing is commonly specified for paper and board.

How Should Laboratories Obtain Reliable Results?

Reliable air-permeability testing requires conditioned specimens, representative sampling, correct pressure, an undamaged seal and a verified instrument. Laboratories should test multiple locations, avoid folds and edges unless required, and record every condition needed to reproduce the measurement.

  1. Condition specimens: Follow the temperature, humidity and duration required by the standard.
  2. Select representative areas: Include expected manufacturing variation.
  3. Verify the test head: Confirm its area and measuring range.
  4. Inspect the seal: Prevent air from bypassing the specimen.
  5. Apply the required pressure: Do not select pressure solely for convenience.
  6. Repeat measurements: Calculate the mean and investigate unusual variation.
  7. Record traceability: Identify the instrument, operator, batch and calibration status.

Test-head area can influence repeatability. A small head may be dominated by one large opening, whereas a larger area averages more of an irregular structure.

Calibration establishes measurement traceability, while routine verification checks whether the instrument continues to operate acceptably between calibrations.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common air-permeability questions concern test principles, pressure, reporting units, standards and material suitability. These answers distinguish direct-flow testing from Gurley testing and identify the information required for reproducible material evaluation.

What does an Air Permeability Tester measure?

It measures the rate of airflow through a defined material area at a specified pressure difference.

Does higher air permeability mean better performance?

Not necessarily. High permeability may benefit breathable clothing but may be undesirable in barrier packaging or filtration media.

What is the difference between porosity and permeability?

Porosity describes the void space inside a material. Permeability describes how readily air travels through connected pores.

Why must the test pressure be reported?

Airflow changes with pressure difference. Results obtained at different pressures should not be assumed to be directly comparable.

Can one tester evaluate textiles and paper?

Some direct-flow instruments support multiple materials, but Gurley paper testing uses a different measurement principle. The required standard must be confirmed first.

What does a high Gurley value mean?

It means a fixed air volume required more time to cross the specimen, indicating lower permeability under that method.

Is air permeability the same as water-vapour permeability?

No. Air permeability measures bulk airflow through pores, while water-vapour permeability measures moisture-vapour transmission.
